# Data

## Metrics and collections: choose the right structure

Do not automatically give every number or item a separate card. Identify the user's decision, the amount of information, the need to compare, the available actions and the expected number of items; then choose a structure that fits. Remove metrics that do not help that decision and supply units, periods or comparisons when needed to interpret the value. Make labels readable and do not manufacture trends or context.

**Metric examples:**

1. **Grouped summary strip:** For a few related values that users compare at a glance, use one shared surface or an unboxed row with consistent columns and subtle separators. Put each readable label close to its value. Example: active projects, in-progress tasks and completed tasks. Wrap or stack on small screens.
2. **Individual metric cards:** Use separate cards only when each metric needs meaningful independent context, an associated chart, status, explanation or action, or when visual emphasis is justified. Example: a balance with a time comparison and an actionable spending alert alongside it. A single number and tiny label alone rarely justify a large isolated block.

**Project and other collection examples:**

1. **List or table:** Use rows when people need to scan or compare similarly structured items, especially as item counts grow. A project row can show name, status, progress and due date, with a real link or button. Use a semantic table if column relationships are important; use a list when the rows are primarily navigation. Reflow at narrow widths.
2. **Cards or a board:** Use cards when items contain meaningful distinct information, images or per-item actions; use a board when moving items between workflow stages is central. Keep cards compact and avoid adding filler simply to occupy space. A card grid is a choice, not a default.

Check real and long content, empty and large collections, keyboard operation, text contrast, responsive widths and zoom. [Carbon's dashboard guidance](https://v10.carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/dashboards/) recommends prioritizing information and limiting distracting metrics. [GOV.UK's summary list guidance](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/summary-list/) distinguishes key-value summaries, tables, and summary cards, and advises against cards for small amounts of related information. [GOV.UK's task list](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/task-list/) shows a different structure for actionable work. These are patterns to choose between, not mandates to copy their appearance.

## Tables: completeness and stable pagination

- Define the columns for each table or compact preview. Every data row must contain the same columns in the same order as its headings; give action columns an accessible label. Check previews and full pages separately, including every row, empty results, filters, and dynamic data. Remember that a map callback also receives an index and array; do not let these become unintended renderer options.
- Keep headings aligned with their values at every viewport. Give wide tables a horizontally scrollable container instead of silently hiding columns or squeezing text until it is unreadable. Use semantic table headings and cells where possible. Make overflow keyboard accessible.
- For paginated tables, reserve space for the selected page size so pagination stays in place on shorter pages and filtered results. Base the space on the header and typical row height, such as a header plus seven roughly 55–56 px rows. Show empty states in the reserved area. Use a minimum height and allow the container to grow with wrapping, zoom, and accessibility text spacing; never clip content to enforce a fixed height.
- Exceptions are appropriate when a table is unpaginated, content height varies substantially, the viewport is small, or reserved space would make an empty view awkward. Verify first and last pages, empty and filtered results, narrow screens, keyboard scrolling, and 200% zoom.

## Charts and data visualizations

- Choose a chart that answers a real question. Make the essential interaction useful, such as switching period, showing exact values on focus/hover, filtering, or expanding a data table. Do not add drag, zoom, animation or controls simply to make a chart interactive. Provide keyboard-operable controls, visible focus, meaningful labels, and a text summary or accessible data table with the same values; hover alone must never be the only way to obtain information. Donuts, sparklines and progress indicators may use a clear adjacent text equivalent where that already communicates the data.
- Size charts to their container using responsive width and a deliberate height or aspect ratio. Give the plot sufficient internal margins for axis labels, ticks and units; account for the longest label and maximum value rather than hardcoding a tiny gutter. Reserve space for legends and controls. Avoid clipping, overlap, excess empty plot area and horizontal page overflow. On narrow screens reduce tick density or offer a horizontally scrollable chart with keyboard access instead of shrinking labels into illegibility. Do not distort SVG aspect ratios.
- Size and contrast chart text as meaningful data, typically at least 14 px for app chart labels, with adequate spacing and legible units. A viewBox that scales down also scales its text: verify its actual rendered size, not only SVG font-size. Show distinct, unambiguous date labels; use consistent numeric formats and enough padding between axes and labels. Check text contrast (normally 4.5:1), and required visual marks against adjacent colors (3:1); do not rely on color alone to distinguish series. Use dashes, labels, patterns or a data table as appropriate.
- No glows, neon shadows or decorative effects. Use restrained palette colors, simple grid lines and clear marks. On load or data change, use chart-specific motion as described below; do not animate on every incidental redraw or obscure updated data. Honor prefers-reduced-motion and show the final state immediately when motion is reduced.
- Verify actual data and scales, zero/baseline choices, long labels and values, empty/loading/error states, multiple viewport widths, 200% zoom, keyboard focus, contrast, and reduced motion. Test each visualization type separately: a pie or progress bar does not need invented axes, and a purely decorative sparkline must not be the sole carrier of important information.


**Chart-specific motion:** A generic fade alone does not show how a chart is built. On initial reveal, grow vertical bars from their baseline (horizontal bars from their baseline horizontally), reveal line or area marks in their reading direction, and sweep pie/donut segments from an empty track to the actual proportions. Keep axes and labels still. On data updates, interpolate matching marks from old values to new values where the chart architecture supports it; otherwise a short, truthful re-entry is acceptable. Start around 400–650 ms with ease-out and little or no stagger. Never bounce, loop, glow, overshoot values or imply fabricated data. Respect reduced motion by rendering the final state immediately. Avoid restarting animations on unrelated re-renders, and test entry, actual data changes, different signs/baselines and rapid updates. Use a chart library when accurate transitions, accessibility, complex scales, or maintainability exceed what a custom SVG can reliably support; small static datasets can use simple SVG and CSS. See [Apache ECharts data transitions](https://echarts.apache.org/handbook/en/how-to/animation/transition/) and [W3C reduced motion technique](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/css/C39).


**Chart rendering checks:** A numeric SVG viewBox font size is not a fixed CSS pixel size: it scales with the SVG. At wide and narrow viewport sizes, inspect rendered axis-label size and weight; let the plot use its available width while keeping labels at a stable readable CSS size, using responsive chart layout or adjusting SVG text for viewBox scaling. Reduce tick density or provide horizontal scrolling when necessary. Ensure SVG text has `stroke: none` when parent SVG styles apply strokes, and set a regular label weight explicitly. Keep the plot proportional to its card without unnecessary scaling. Inspect legends next to the actual chart: line-series swatches should be short lines, with a dashed line for a dashed series. Use chart-specific class names rather than generic button classes such as `secondary`; reset padding, minimum size and borders on swatches so button styles cannot turn them into large rectangles. Test the actual rendered chart and legend at wide and narrow widths, with focus, zoom and data controls.


**Page navigation:** When the destination changes, give the new content a quick, restrained entrance: start around 150–200 ms ease-out with a subtle fade and at most a few pixels of movement. Swap content immediately so navigation, focus and assistive technology are not delayed; keep persistent navigation stationary. Animate only actual page changes, not filtering, pagination or routine data updates. Cancel an in-progress entrance on rapid navigation and show the latest destination. Respect prefers-reduced-motion by displaying the final state immediately. Check keyboard focus, scroll position, rapid navigation, and loading or error states; do not use motion to disguise a slow load.

**Expandable sections:** Use a real disclosure button or native details/summary with an accessible name and accurate expanded state. Use a centered, background-free SVG or CSS chevron that points right when closed and down when open, rotating smoothly around its center in roughly 150–200 ms. On opening, animate the panel's height from zero to its natural content height with a short ease-out (about 180–250 ms); avoid fixed maximum heights that clip long or zoomed content. Where intrinsic-size animation is unsupported, show all content and use a short opacity/position entrance instead. Do not move focus, suppress keyboard operation or hide content from assistive technology incorrectly. Honor reduced motion by showing the final state immediately. Apply this to disclosures throughout the product, including chart data, FAQs and expandable navigation, not only charts. Verify long content, rapid toggles, responsive layouts, keyboard and reduced motion.

## Data-aware panel layout

Choose the layout from real content and user tasks, not from a fixed set of equal boxes. Use this decision process for dashboards and other collections:

1. **Inventory the data first.** For each section identify the user's question, required values and actions, typical and maximum item counts, longest labels and values, whether data updates, and loading, empty and error states. Distinguish actual content from decoration. Never invent or duplicate data to fill a panel.
2. **Estimate the space each section needs.** At each intended width, render real representative content with its heading, controls, chart labels, list rows and footer. Let text wrap. Compare natural heights and useful widths; include chart minimum legible dimensions and accessible targets. A shared grid row stretches shorter panels by default, so identify whether an oversized panel is caused by stretching, a fixed height, padding, chart dimensions or a large minimum height before changing it.
3. **Choose the structure.** Group related, comparable measures in a compact strip, table or list; use individual cards when each has a distinct task, chart or meaningful detail. Place related information together and prioritize important information earlier. Consider wider/narrower columns according to content needs, a single column on narrow screens, or independent content-height panels when equal heights provide no benefit. Avoid a rigid uniform card grid simply for symmetry.
4. **Handle spare space deliberately.** If aligned panel bottoms help scanning and a panel has a meaningful footer or summary, keep its content together near the top and place that footer at the bottom using a column flex layout and auto margin; retain a minimum gap before it. This distributes unavoidable extra space without inventing content. If the footer must remain next to a specific value or the gap becomes misleading, keep them together and allow the panel to fit its content instead. Do not stretch charts, increase type, add fillers, or spread every row merely to occupy space. A bottom-aligned note may still leave space above it; change the grid if that space harms the result.
5. **Validate with data and interaction.** Compare zero, one, typical and many items; long or localized labels; expanded chart data; errors, loading and dynamic updates; mobile and wide screens; 200% zoom and keyboard use. Verify no clipping, artificial whitespace, overlap, misleading grouping, lost data, or unstable movement when content changes. Avoid fixed heights unless a specific behavior such as stable pagination requires one, and still allow for text enlargement. Space is useful when it clarifies groups; the goal is a balanced, readable layout, not maximum density.

These are project heuristics informed by [Carbon's dashboard guidance](https://www.carbondesignsystem.com/building-blocks/data-visualization/dashboards), [GOV.UK dashboards](https://brand.design-system.service.gov.uk/data/dashboards/), [Atlassian's grid guidance](https://atlassian.design/foundations/grid/), and [MDN's explanation of grid alignment and stretching](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Guides/Grid_layout/Box_alignment); the sources do not prescribe a universal optimal density or fixed whitespace threshold.
